Why the same shot isn't the same shot Two players can stand the same distance apart and be in completely different fights. This is a visual guide to the geometry that decides which one you are in.

You can already see this. You probably cannot say it.

Most strong players can look at a duel and tell you it feels wrong. Far fewer can say which feature of the map made it wrong, and that is a real cost. Intuition you cannot articulate does not survive a change of map, cannot be argued about with a teammate, cannot be reviewed from a recording, and tends to go quiet exactly when you have time to use it.

The two duels below are identical in every way a scoreboard would notice. Same models, same range, same weapons. They are not the same fight.

On the left, Red can step anywhere. On the right, half of those steps end in a wall, and Blue is holding a much easier shot for it. Nothing about the players changed. The map did the work.

That difference has parts, and the parts have names. The rest of this page introduces them one at a time, each after you have seen what it does. If you would rather have the vocabulary up front, it is in the glossary, but the terms are much easier to keep once you have watched them earn their place.

Two different questions

This page answers the second one.

The first question is should I take this fight at all. That depends on the objective, your cooldowns, where your team is, who else is about to arrive, and what happens if you lose. Geometry has nothing useful to say about any of it, and it never overrules it. A geometrically perfect position on the wrong side of the map is still the wrong side of the map.

The second question is given that I am fighting this person, how favourable is the geometry. That is local, it lasts a fraction of a second, and it transfers between games almost unchanged. It is the only thing here.

The two are not independent, and the first constrains the second rather than the other way round. There is one useful check that sits between them: if the fight cannot be won even assuming every single shot lands, no amount of positioning rescues it, and you should be changing the fight instead of improving your angle. That is worth a moment before any of the geometry is worth anything.

Where he can be, not where he is

The unit of a fight is a region, not a point.

By the time your shot arrives, the person you aimed at has moved. So the thing worth thinking about is not where he is standing but everywhere he could be a moment from now. That is a region around him, and its size is set by how fast he moves and how long you need.

We need a short window for this to be meaningful. It has to be short enough that neither player gets to re-plan inside it, which puts it somewhere around a quarter to a third of a second. This site uses roughly 300 ms as a reference interval. Nothing important happens at exactly 300 and stops happening at 301: the number is a convenient scale, not a boundary.

Call that region his player-dome, his reachable space. In the open it is a disc, because tapping movement keys in combination fills it in continuously rather than leaving eight spokes. If the game strafes slower than it runs, it is an ellipse instead.

Try it. Change the movement speed and the window, and watch the region grow. Then drag Red against the wall and watch what the wall takes away from him.

A wall does not slow him down. It removes places he was allowed to be, which is a different and more useful thing to notice.

You have one too. Everything computed for the enemy exists for you, and it is drawn in blue on every figure here. Its own name is the self-dome, and it gets one because forgetting it is the single most common way to talk yourself into a bad position. From here on, one principle applies to everything on this page:

Principle

Any geometric advantage has to survive swapping the cameras. If it looks good from your side and equally good from his, you have not gained anything.

Cover changes where he can move

The first of the two things an obstacle does.

Put a wall behind someone and some of his movement keys stop working. A key that runs into a wall is not a direction any more, and one that runs along the wall still is, because he slides rather than stops. Call the ones that still work his free directions.

Here is the part that surprises people. He is facing you, so his keys are oriented by your position. Walk around him and you change which of his inputs still function, without him doing anything at all.

Try it. Drag the viewing angle slowly and watch the count. Pay attention around 45°.

The count is a staircase rather than a curve, and it steps back up at every exact multiple of 45°, where two of his inputs land parallel to the wall and slide along it. In between, he is down to four.

That last detail is worth being careful about. It is a property of movement built from eight fixed inputs, which is how these games work, but it is not a fact about geometry, and it would soften in a game with analogue movement. The transferable part is not the number 45. It is that an obstacle behind someone costs him movement, that the cost depends on where you stand, and that a player holding a blocked key loses speed and becomes easier to hit.

The same movement can look different from another angle

The second thing an obstacle does, and the one people skip.

Your aim does not respond to how far someone travels through the map. It responds to how far he travels across your screen. A player crossing four metres at long range barely moves your crosshair. The same four metres at close range is a flick.

So a reachable space has two separate sizes worth tracking. How big it is in the map, which is a fact about walls and movement speed. And how big it looks from a particular camera, which is a fact about where you are standing. The second is its apparent surface, its screen-space size.

Try it. Walk Blue around Red at a fixed range. Watch the two bars: one is measured in the map, the other on Blue's screen.

The region on the map never changes. The region on the screen collapses by half. Red has done nothing, and Blue's shot has become substantially easier.

This is where a lot of positioning talk goes wrong, so it is worth keeping the two words apart. When an obstacle takes away somewhere he could stand, his reachable space got smaller. When your camera moves and the same reachable space covers less of your monitor, its apparent surface got smaller. Both make your shot easier. Only the first survives him looking somewhere else.

What you can hit, and where he goes instead

The most useful split on the whole page.

Take the reachable space as it appears on your screen and divide it in two. Part of it is currently filled by his body. The rest is empty, and it is where he can be next.

The body is what you hit. Yellow is where they can move instead.

The body is his model-dome, the hittable area, and you want it large. The yellow is his empty-dome, his movement room, and you want it small, because it is what your crosshair has to be able to chase. Between them they account for the whole thing.

Try it. Drag Blue around and watch the two bars trade against each other. Then swap the camera and check what Red is getting.

These two do not move together

It would be convenient if shrinking someone's movement room always made him easier to kill. It does not, and the reason is the most practically important thing on this page.

Cover can pin a player almost completely while leaving only his head showing. His movement is nearly gone and so is anything to shoot at. He is predictable and very hard to hit. In the open, the opposite: the whole body is available and so is every direction he could take it.

So there is a genuine tension, not a single quantity to maximise. Which side of it you want depends on what actually beats your aim. If small targets beat you, you want the body. If fast movement beats you, you want the room. Most positions offer some of one in exchange for some of the other, and that trade is what a good position is made of.

The direction his movement looks widest

A measurement to navigate by, not an instruction.

Walk a full circle around someone at a fixed range, measuring how much of his reachable space is on your screen at every bearing. You get a curve. Where that curve peaks is the direction from which his movement appears widest, and it is worth a name because everything else is described relative to it. Call it a normal, a reference direction.

It is a maximum, so it is where his movement is hardest for you to cover. Moving away from it reduces what you have to watch. Whether that is an improvement depends entirely on what the same move does to your own geometry, which is why this is a reference and not advice.

Try it. Switch between the four situations and watch the shape of the curve, not just its peak.

Against a wall there is one peak, square to it. In a corner there is one, along the diagonal. Behind a freestanding rock there are two, one per side. In the open the curve is flat, which is the precise way of saying there is no angle to take and you should stop looking for one.

Two curves, not one. The solid curve is what you can see. The dashed one is the shape of his reachable space by itself. A wall behind him moves both, because it removes places he can stand. A doorway in front of him moves only the solid one, because it does not restrict his feet at all, it just hides them. Watching them come apart in the doorway case is the clearest way to see that an obstacle can be doing either job, or both.

Taking an angle, then, is not simply standing diagonally. It means using the obstacle against his movement without handing the same obstacle equal leverage over yours. That is why the extremes never work: push far enough and you are the one against the wall.

Both cameras at once

Everything so far, assembled into one comparison.

You now have two quantities and they exist on both sides, which makes four numbers. Written as a sentence it is unreadable, so do not read it as a sentence. Read it as two screens.

Your screen
More of his body. Less of his movement.

His screen
Less of your body. More of your movement.

When both comparisons point your way, the position is good. When both point the other way, it is bad, and the fact that you can see him clearly does not save it. When they disagree you are looking at a trade, and which half you want is the same question as before: are you beaten by small targets or by fast movement.

Try it. Move Blue off the square line and watch all four bars. The useful habit is to check the right-hand panel before you decide anything.

Notice what happens when Blue stands directly in front of the wall. Every bar is identical. Red is the one with a wall behind him and it has bought Blue nothing at all, because the part of Red's movement the wall removed was never going to appear on Blue's screen from there. That is the clearest demonstration of the principle: it looked like an advantage until the second camera was checked.

Position quality is not shot quality

These are different things and it is worth separating them explicitly. Current exposure is what you can hit right now. Movement potential is how quickly that can change.

A moment can offer a very easy shot from a structurally poor position, because he happens to be visible and is about to have five good options. A strong position can offer no shot at all for a second and then a free one. If you judge positions by the shot available in the instant you looked, you will keep choosing the first over the second.

How much angle, then?

Sweeping every angle at a fixed range against a single wall gives a curve with a shape worth remembering, even if you never remember a number from it.

It is zero at both ends, for the same reason each time. Square to the wall the two screens are identical. Flat against the wall yourself they are identical again. Everything useful happens strictly in between, and the middle is broad, which means there is no single correct angle to memorise and small errors cost you very little.

Under these movement assumptions and this geometry. Change the movement model, the range, or what beats your aim, and the peak moves. The shape does not.

A corner opens faster when you stand close to it

The mechanism behind every peeking situation.

Stand close to a corner and a single sideways step swings your sight line a long way past it, uncovering a lot of hidden ground at once. Stand well back and the same step swings it barely at all. The far player therefore uncovers the space gradually, and can stop at any point.

Try it. Move Blue further from the corner while leaving Red where he is, and compare how much new ground each player exposes with the same step.

Whoever stands further from the corner reveals less per step, and therefore chooses how much to show and when. The other player is committing.

This matters even when neither player can shoot yet, which is most of a corner standoff. Nobody has a hittable area. What is actually being contested is control over the reveal, and distance is how you win it.

It is not a universal rule about cover. That is the part to be careful with. This works when the obstacle sits between you and the enemy, controlling what you can see of each other. When the obstacle is instead hiding your own body, staying close to it is what keeps you hidden, and backing off simply exposes you. A raised ledge is the standard example, and the guidance there inverts completely.

Transferable rule

Distance from cover is not good or bad in itself. Ask whose sightline the cover is controlling.

If it is between the two of you, standing back buys you finer control of the reveal. If it is hiding your body, standing close is what reduces your exposure. The same wall does opposite jobs depending on which side of it you are standing.

What to actually do in a game

Five questions, in order.

The whole framework compresses into a short sequence you can run in the moment. It is not a calculation. It is a way of looking at a position that reliably surfaces the thing that matters.

One question per term
Reachable space
Where can he be in the next moment?
Screen-space size
How much of that appears on my screen?
Hittable area
How much target do I get?
Movement room
How much room can he move into?
Reference
From which side does his movement look widest?
Your own
What does he see when I ask all of this about me?
Where can he move?Not where is he. Where could he be a moment from now.
What is restricting it?A wall, an edge, a doorway. Is it stopping his feet, or stopping my eyes, or both.
How does that look from here?How much body do I get, and how much room does he have on my screen.
What does he see of me?The same two questions, from his camera. This is the one people skip.
Can I change angle or distance so his options shrink faster than mine?If not, the position is even, whatever it feels like.

You are not supposed to be calculating any of this while playing. Nobody estimates screen-space areas mid-fight, and trying would be worse than useless. The model exists to train your eye. After enough deliberate looking, the questions stop being questions: you see that the wall behind him is doing nothing from where you are standing, the way you currently see that someone is reloading.

The place to do the deliberate looking is a recording. Find a duel that felt wrong, rebuild it, swap the cameras, and check whether the position was actually asymmetric or whether you simply lost an even fight. Those are different problems with different fixes, and telling them apart is most of what improvement is.

Ten situations, six shapes

Almost every position in a shooter is one of a handful of geometric primitives, or two of them stacked. Recognising which one you are in is most of the work.

Each of these gives you the situation, asks you to commit to an answer before you look, lets you change it, shows what moved, checks that the idea transferred, and ends with one sentence worth taking to a different map. Answer the questions before you touch the figure. Getting one wrong is more useful than reading the explanation first.

The lab

For rebuilding something you actually saw.

Every figure above is first person, which is the case the geometry is cleanest in. The lab is where you can change that, along with movement speed, weapon spread and the rest. Maths and implementation

Nothing below this line is needed to use anything above it.

Every term, formally

Each of these was introduced by picture earlier. Here is the same thing written down.

Reachable space→ D = { q : |q − p| ≤ v·Δt, q reachable without passing a solid }
Screen-space size→ Ω = ∫∫ dΩ over the projection, in steradians
Hittable area / movement room→ Ω_dome = Ω_model + Ω_empty, a partition of the visible projection
Reference→ θ_N = argmax over θ of Ω_dome(θ), at fixed range
Reveal control→ dA/dδ ≈ L² / 2d
The comparison→ Ω_model(him) > Ω_model(you) and Ω_empty(him) < Ω_empty(you)

Apparent surface

Screen-space size is solid angle. For a pinhole camera with the image plane at unit distance and coordinates (a, b), a patch of the image subtends

dΩ = da · db / (1 + a² + b²)^{3/2}

The solver renders the scene into a small framebuffer, tags every pixel as sky, obstacle, model or reachable space, and sums that weight over the pixels carrying each tag. The viewport pictures on this page are that framebuffer, so the picture and the measurement cannot disagree.

Reference directions

Finding one is a one-dimensional optimisation: sweep the bearing at fixed range and take the maximisers of Ω. The set of maximisers is generally an arc rather than a single ray, which is why the figures draw arcs. When the curve is flat to within a small tolerance, every direction qualifies and no reference direction is reported.

Reveal control

Standing a distance d from a corner and stepping sideways by δ rotates the sight line past the corner by about δ/d. Sweeping that angle across ground extending a distance L beyond the corner uncovers roughly

ΔA ≈ ½ L² · δ/d, so dA/dδ ≈ L² / 2d

The measured curve in the figure above is two visibility grids differenced, and it follows the inverse relationship closely enough that the approximation is doing no work the geometry was not already doing.

What is measured, what is assumed

One possible aim model

The geometry above stands on its own and does not depend on this. What follows is one way of showing that a larger hittable area and a smaller movement room can jointly improve your outcome, rather than a claim about how aiming works.

Suppose shots scatter with angular error σ, and that σ grows with the room the target has to move into. For a circular target of solid angle Ω the chance a shot lands is

P = 1 − exp( −Ω / 2πσ² ), σ² = σ²_spread + ( c · √(Ω_empty/π) )²

Expected damage then rises with the hittable area and falls with the movement room, for every choice of parameter. That is the sense in which the two halves of the comparison belong together rather than being two unrelated rules of thumb.

The constant c is how much of the opponent's available room actually defeats your tracking, and it is what decides which half of the comparison you should weight. Setting it to zero recovers the perfect-aim case, where only the hittable area matters.

The absolute numbers this produces are not meaningful and are not used anywhere on the player-facing part of the site.

Implementation

A software rasteriser with a depth buffer and an id buffer, about five hundred lines, no dependencies. Reachable space is stored as a height field over a grid, so obstacles clip it exactly and the same structure serves the map view, the viewport and the sweep.

Every obstacle is a convex polyhedron held both as half-spaces, for containment and ray clipping, and as faces, for rasterising. Boxes and ramps come from the same representation, which is why sloped ground needs no special case anywhere else.

The bearing sweep and the advantage field run in a worker. The field evaluates two full comparisons per map cell, each with its own pair of reachable spaces, and masks any cell where neither player can see the other.

Take this away

A fight has two cameras. Around each player is a short-term region of possible movement. Cover changes that region, and perspective changes how large it looks. Good local geometry makes the enemy easier to expose and easier to track while preserving your own ability to hide and move. Angles and distance are useful because they can make those two views unequal.
