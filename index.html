Orientation

A very small vocabulary

Almost everything worth saying about geometric positioning can be said with six words. Where a player can be in the next fraction of a second is his player-dome. How much of that lands on your monitor is its apparent surface. The part of it his body is covering right now is the model-dome, and the part he can still move into is the empty-dome. The direction that makes his dome look as large as possible is a normal. A movement key that does not run into a wall is a free direction.

That is the whole toolkit. The heuristic in §2.3 is two sentences built out of it, and the ten exercises in §3 are that heuristic applied to a wall, a corner, a rock, a doorway, a ledge and a slope. Nothing more exotic ever appears.

What makes those six words worth the trouble is that they are all measurable. An apparent surface is a solid angle, and a solid angle is a number of steradians. A normal is whatever direction maximises that number, so finding one is an optimisation rather than a matter of taste. A free direction is a collision test. None of this has to be pictured or argued about, and on this page none of it is: every viewport renders the scene into a buffer, tags each pixel as obstacle, model or dome, and adds up the solid angle of each tag. The orange region is the model-dome. The yellow region is the empty-dome.

Two habits will get you most of the value. First, read every number twice, once for him and once for you, because the heuristic is a comparison and half of it is always about your own dome. Second, drag things. The geometry is far more sensitive to a metre of standoff than anyone expects, and the fastest way to learn that is to watch a number move.

1. Introduction

Two kinds of positioning

Only one of them is geometry, and it is the one nobody can explain out loud.

Everyone uses the word positioning and nobody separates its parts. Good players have decent intuition about what is a good or a bad position, but that intuition is phenomenological and brute forced. Ask someone to judge a concrete situation and you get "good" or "bad" with no account of why. That is a real cost. Intuition does not survive a change of map, it cannot be argued about, it is nearly impossible to hand to someone else, and it tends to go quiet exactly when you have time to use it.

Split positioning in two. Game sense positioning is objective driven: rotations, hot spots, routes, when to engage and when to leave. It is global, slow, worth seconds to tens of seconds, and heavily specific to the game you are playing. Mechanical positioning is combat driven: crosshair placement, target selection, dodging, and abusing the map geometry to hit for free or to avoid being hit for free. That last one is geometric positioning, it is universal, and it is the only thing on this page.

Game sense always takes precedence. Your primary goal is to win the game. Being efficient inside a fight is a secondary goal, and it is constrained by the first one. Which means that before any of the geometry below is worth a single millisecond of your attention, the fight has to be worth taking at all. There is a blunt test for that.

The aggressive aimbot criterion. A decision to take a fight, or a way to take it, is bad if you have no way to win it even by assuming you have an aimbot.

The passive aimbot criterion. A decision to avoid a fight, or a way to avoid it, is bad if you have no way to lose it even by assuming your enemy has an aimbot.

Quoted from §1

It sounds trivial. It is not, and the guide's own example is the reason. You have 157 health and deal 100 damage per second. You are looking at two players with 100 and 50 health who each deal 80. You have more health than either of them and a better damage output than either of them. Select targets correctly, kill the 50 first, and you are still dead before the second one falls.

Work it yourself in the panel opposite. Change any number and the verdict moves. What you are looking for is the moment where the arithmetic says no and your instincts still say yes, because that is the fight you have been losing for years.

2.1 Two dimensional and three dimensional aim

Your monitor is flat

The depth you feel in a game is a story your brain is telling you. Refusing to listen is a skill.

A shooter happens in three dimensions but you look at it through one first person window, which puts it much closer to two. You have two eyes a few centimetres apart, and that is where real depth comes from. Take a pen in one hand and its cap in the other and cap it with both eyes open. Easy. Now close one eye and try again. That difference is the whole content of the phrase "3d aim", and your monitor gives you none of it.

3d aim is what you do without trying. You aim at models moving through a space, you feel the depth, and you move the crosshair accordingly. It is very good for predicting movement. It also makes some easy shots hard, and it insists that two identical pictures are different situations. A head coming up over a rock three metres away and a target of the same apparent size a hundred metres away can land on exactly the same pixels of your screen, and your brain will refuse to treat them the same way.

2d aim, or projective aim, is what you do when you force the depth back out of the picture. It is unnatural, it costs real attention, and you cannot hold it all the time. It is excellent for pre-shooting, for crossing corners, and for flicking. It is bad at predicting movement, because you have stopped aiming at a player and started aiming at a point in space. If you are good at 1wall6targets, you already have it.

The cube is the standard demonstration. From above, from below, or twelve line segments on a flat surface. All three readings are legitimate and none of them is correct. Forcing the third one is harder than the other two, and it is the reading every measurement on this page uses.

2.2 Player-disk and player-dome

Where he can be in 300 milliseconds

The unit of a fight is not a position. It is the set of positions he can reach before anything you decide now stops being true.

Fix a time scale first. Fights run on the order of 300 milliseconds: long enough to matter, short enough that neither of you gets to re-plan in the middle of it. Over that window a player who runs at speed v in eight directions can reach anywhere inside a disk of radius 300 ms × v. That is the player-disk. Not where he is. Where he can be.

Eight keys are not eight destinations. Tap them in combination and you fill the disk continuously, which is why it is a disk and not an octagon. If the game gives you slower strafe speed than forward speed the boundary becomes an ellipse instead, and if backward speed differs too it becomes something uglier. It stays a player-disk regardless.

Now let him jump and crouch and the disk becomes a volume, the player-dome. It is his accessible space over 300 milliseconds, which is to say it is his freedom. When nothing intersects it, it is free.

And you have one. It is worth saying twice, because almost every mistake in the exercises later is somebody forgetting it: you have a player-dome too, your self-dome, and most of what shrinks his also shrinks yours. Move the sliders and watch how little of this is fixed by the game you happen to be playing.

2.2 Obstacles and free directions

Why 45° is not an optimal angle

The most checkable claim in the guide, and the one that convinced me the whole framework was worth computing.

Push an obstacle into a dome and it stops being free. A player with his back against a wall has half a dome. A player in a corner has a quarter. The interesting part is that he is facing you, so the eight keys under his fingers are oriented by where you stand. Walk around him and you change which of his keys still work.

A free direction is one of the eight that does not run into an obstacle when he holds it. Count them as you orbit an enemy pinned to an infinite wall and you do not get a curve, you get a staircase. At every exact multiple of 45° two of his keys land tangent to the wall. They slide along it instead of into it, so they still count, and he keeps five. At any other angle he keeps four.

That is the answer to the exercise, and it is not an aesthetic point. 45° looks like a sensible compromise and is nothing of the kind: it is a spike where your enemy gets a key back for free. 65° is strictly worse for him, and the reason is physical rather than notional. Hold a blocked key too long and you hit the wall, lose ground speed, and become easier to hit.

The staircase below is measured, not drawn. For every bearing the solver marches all eight of his keys against the actual geometry and reports how far each one gets before something stops it. Drag him off the wall and watch the staircase flatten to eight.

2.2 The normal

A normal is an argmax

Which is convenient, because it means you can solve for one.

At fixed distance there exists a (non-necessarily unique) direction from your perspective that maximizes the apparent surface of the player-dome. Such a direction is called a normal.

Quoted from §2.2

Take that at its word and finding a normal is a one dimensional optimisation. Walk a circle around your enemy at fixed range, measure how much of his dome is on your monitor at every bearing, and read off the maxima. That curve is the Positioning Rose, and it is the main instrument here.

Three claims fall straight out of it. Against a wall there is one maximum, perpendicular to the wall, and the curve is r(1 + cos θ): twice as wide head on as from the side. In a corner there is one maximum and it sits on the angle bisector. In the open the curve is flat, which is the formal way of saying there is nothing to take an angle on and you should stop looking for one.

One refinement the guide does not make. The word "normal" is quietly doing two jobs. An obstacle can shrink the apparent surface of a dome by stopping him moving into it, or by stopping you seeing where he moves, and those are not the same obstacle. A wall behind him does the first. A doorway in front of him does only the second. The rose plots both curves: the solid one is what you can see, the dashed one is the shape of his reachable space alone. Where they agree, the guide is unambiguous. Where they part, so do its answers, and The Opening is where that first shows up.

Angles here are bearings from your enemy to you, measured anticlockwise from his nearest normal, which is the sign convention of §4.3.

2.2 Model-dome and empty-dome

Orange is what you hit. Yellow is where he goes instead.

Two numbers, both in steradians, both on your monitor right now.

Split the apparent surface of his player-dome in two. The part his model currently covers is the model-dome. That is what you aim at, and bigger is better for you. Everything left over is the empty-dome: space he can move into but is not occupying. That is what you have to chase with your crosshair, and smaller is better for you.

These viewports are the measurement itself. The solver renders the scene at low resolution, tags every pixel as obstacle, model or dome, and sums the solid angle of each tag. The orange pixels are the model-dome. The yellow pixels are the empty-dome. There is no separate calculation happening off screen that these pictures are meant to represent.

Both are shown from both chairs, always, because the situation is symmetric and pretending otherwise is how you lose. He is looking at your model-dome and your empty-dome while you look at his. Every judgement in this framework is a comparison of four numbers. It is never an inspection of two.

The unit is the millisteradian. For scale, a 103° viewport is about 2700 msr in total, and a player standing 15 metres away is roughly 4 msr, which is about a fifth of one per cent of your screen. That is the target. The rest is where he might be instead.

2.3 The heuristic about geometric positioning

Four numbers, two inequalities

The heuristic

In a fight, a positioning is good if the apparent surface of the model-dome of your enemy, seen from your perspective, is bigger than the apparent surface of your model-dome seen from his, and if the apparent surface of the empty-dome of your enemy, seen from your perspective, is smaller than the apparent surface of your empty-dome seen from his.

A good compromise to achieve this is to take an angle on one of the normals of his player-dome, without letting him take an angle on one normal of your self-dome.

That is the whole thing. Make him easier to hit than you are, and give yourself more room to move than he has. Drag either player on the map and both clauses resolve live, along with the compromise between them.

Why it is true

The guide states the heuristic and does not derive it, which is fair enough. It is a heuristic. But the two clauses are not two independent rules of thumb that happen to sit next to each other. They are the two partial derivatives of the same quantity, and it is worth seeing that.

Take the smallest honest model of a fight. Your shots scatter with some angular error σ, and that error grows with the amount of space the target has to move into. For a target of apparent solid angle ΩM the chance a shot lands is 1 − exp(−ΩM / 2πσ²), with σ² = σ²spread + (c · √(ΩE/π))². Expected damage rises with the model-dome and falls with the empty-dome. Always, for every choice of parameter. The heuristic's two clauses are precisely those two signs, and the solver reports them numerically rather than asserting them.

The model also explains why the guide refuses to rank the two clauses, and why you should not either. Their relative weight is that constant c: how much of your enemy's available space actually beats your tracking. A precise but unreactive player should care more about the empty-dome. A reactive but imprecise one should care more about the model-dome. The slider is that constant, and moving it changes which positions the solver prefers. Set it to zero and you have an aimbot, at which point only the model-dome matters and the heuristic collapses to "get closer".

So which angle, exactly?

Exercise 3.1 asks for at least two optimal angles against an infinite wall, and answers plus or minus 65°. That is a claim about a curve, so here is the curve: the heuristic score at every angle, at whatever range you like.

It has the shape the guide describes and it nails both of the negative answers exactly. On the normal the score is zero. Mirror-symmetry means you have gained nothing at all by standing there. At plus or minus 90° it is zero again, because now you are flat against the wall yourself and the situation is symmetric a second time. Everything useful happens strictly in between, and the maximum is broad, which is why 65° is a good answer rather than the only one.

Note what this curve does not know about. It compares apparent surfaces, which is exactly what the heuristic asks you to compare, but the preference for 65° over, say, 80° also rests on free directions and on not handing him an angle back. Those live in the readouts, not in this plot.

What the heuristic will not do for you

The guide lists its own limits and they are worth keeping in front of you, because most of them are reasons a computed score can be perfectly correct and still lose you the fight.

  • You can almost never maximise his model-dome and minimise his empty-dome at the same time. You have to choose, and the right choice depends on your aim rather than on the geometry.
  • The same applies to your own two domes. The balance that still leaves you ahead is usually found by taking an angle, which is the whole point of the second sentence of the heuristic.
  • Spread punishes the endgame. Reduce his empty-dome to almost nothing and you may have made his model-dome too small to land a shot on.
  • A head-shot multiplier changes the arithmetic of every trade, sometimes by a factor of two.
  • A good enemy expects you to follow the heuristic and pre-shoots where it tells you to be. Peeking a rock with only your head showing is a known position, and known positions get pre-aimed.
  • Dodging turns all of this into a mind game. He can collapse his own empty-dome deliberately, with timing, at exactly the moment you are late on him.
  • Positions update continuously, so the normals update, so what "taking an angle" means updates. That is not really a limit. You can watch it happen and react to it.

4.3 Perspective and revealed surface

Distance from cover is control over cover

The quietest paragraph in the guide, and the one that decides every peeking exercise in it.

By moving around an obstacle nearby, a lot of surface behind it is revealed. When you're far from an obstacle, very few surface behind it is revealed by moving around it. You therefore have much more control about what you want to see the further you are from the corner.

Quoted from §4.3

Stand a distance d from a corner and step sideways by δ. Your sight line past the corner sweeps through an angle of roughly δ/d, so the area you newly expose out to a range L is about ½L²·δ/d. Which gives you a law:

dA/dδ ≈ L² / 2d

Exposure falls off as one over your distance to cover. The purple region on the map is the surface a single step gains, and the chart underneath puts the measured rate against that closed form. They agree, which is satisfying, but the useful part is the consequence.

The player further from the obstacle controls it. He reveals less per step, so he chooses what to show and when. That single fact is why increasing your distance from a corner before you show yourself beats hugging it, why baiting a rock means circling at a wider radius than his, and why the man standing further back from a doorway owns the doorway. The ratio of two players' control over the same corner is just the inverse ratio of their distances to it, so you can estimate it by eye in game.

It has one sharp exception, and the guide flags it as the first of its kind. On a high ground the low player wants to be closer to the wall, not further, because there the obstacle is hiding him rather than hiding what he wants to see. Exercise 3.6 is where the rule inverts. Do not learn the rule without learning the exception.
